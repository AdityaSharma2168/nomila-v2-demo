Seven ways a video starts, one pipeline that finishes it. Every case ends with the
same professional-grade edit, captions and a scheduled post — whether the
footage already exists, has to be generated, needs the athlete to film four
seconds, or is nothing but her photos set in motion.

GROUND RULES
WHAT HOLDS ACROSS ALL SEVEN
01
Plan first, then check coverage. The concept is designed to be good, not designed
around whatever footage happens to exist. Coverage is checked after the plan, never
before it.
02
Her real footage wins whenever quality is comparable. It is free, it is authentically her,
and it sidesteps every likeness problem.
03
The quality bar never moves. Raw phone clips, Instagram downloads, generated shots
and brand-supplied media all get the same finishing pass and come out looking like one
production.
04
Only ask her to film what genuinely needs her — and always offer the alternative:
generate it instead, or swap the scene for one she already has.
05
Brand rules outrank taste. No competitor in frame, correct logo, required disclosure,
tone respected — checked before she ever sees the cut.

06
Nothing spends without her tap. Planning, recommendations, scene breakdowns and
film-it cards are free. Generation is quoted and approved, every time.
07
The agent's choices come from a capability library, not from this page. Every "what the
agent decides" list below is a starting sample. For each scene the agent works through
the full library of camera, light, colour, time, motion, layout and finishing techniques (see
Craft decisions) and picks what serves the concept. It isn't limited to the examples
written here, and the library keeps growing.

BRAND WORK — THE BRIEF IS OPTIONAL
TRIGGER · BRIEF, PRODUCT, BRAND NAME, OR DELIVERABLE DUE
“Give me whatever you have. I'll tell you what it covers and fill the rest.”
One path, not two. A brand video may arrive as a ten-page brief, a box of product with no
instructions, or a line in a deal that says “1 reel”. In every case the agent produces the
same thing — a derived brief, then a concept, then a scene-by-scene plan — and states
plainly which parts it inferred.
Her footage is the base, not a fallback. Whatever arrives, every scene is checked against her
library and anything she attaches before generation is ever proposed — a brand video built
entirely from generated material is the exception, not the default. It is cheaper, it is
recognisably her, and it is what makes the ad read as an athlete's rather than a stock spot.
Generation fills the gaps her footage cannot: product detail, environments she has never
shot, b-roll that would take a day to film.
WHAT IT WORKS FROM WHEN THERE'S NO BRIEF

The concept comes from where the two identities genuinely overlap — jewellery that
stays on through the ordinary hours of an athlete's day beats “here is the product”, and it
is built from data the system already holds.

PHOTOS INTO MOTION — NOTHING GENERATED
NEW · TRIGGER: SHE PICKS PHOTOS, OR A SET OF STILLS FROM ONE DAY
“Twelve photos, one video. Every pixel is hers.”
This case is treatment and editing only. It takes several of her photos and makes a
video from them using editing techniques alone: split screens, zooms, cutouts,
layered effects and transitions cut to music. No model generates or repaints
anything. It is a photo edit that plays as a video. Plenty of athletes have hundreds
of good photos and very little usable video, so this turns the bigger half of their
library into video content.
HOW IT WORKS

Every entry carries the same fields, so the agent can use a new one without code changes: what it
does, when it works (and when it doesn't), what input it needs (a clip, a still, a subject mask, a beat
grid), whether it's editing or generation, its cost and time, the quality risk, and an example. The
agent plans against these fields, never against a hard-coded list.
How a choice gets made. Every choice traces back to something: the concept and feeling, the
brand's rules, the beat grid, what performs for her, or what the source can support. A 480p phone
clip can't take a hard punch-in, and a backlit photo cuts out cleanly. The agent records its reason for
each choice, so she can change any one without the rest of the plan falling apart. It can also save a
combination she liked as her own style, which becomes a new library entry.
APPLIED TO EVERY CASE
THE FINISHING PASS
Raw phone clips, Instagram downloads, generated shots and brand-supplied media all
go through the same pass, so real and generated scenes cut together as one
production. Restraint is part of it — a behind-the-scenes piece keeps its handheld feel,
just cleaner.
STAGEWHAT HAPPENS
AnalyseEvery clip measured: shake, exposure, white balance, noise, focus, tilt, flicker,
subject position, audio levels. Every later decision comes from these numbers.

STAGEWHAT HAPPENS
Clean upStabilise, level the horizon, denoise, deflicker, conform frame rates, upscale low-
resolution footage, light sharpening.
ReframeVertical crop that follows her or the product, punch-ins for emphasis, text-safe
framing clear of the platform's buttons.
ColourCorrect each clip, match every clip to the hero scene, one creative grade from the
look or brand palette, relight anything correction can't save.
MotionSlow motion on peaks, speed ramps into drops, freeze frames, each clip trimmed to
its strongest moment.
AssembleCuts on the beat, transitions chosen by the change in energy, match cuts where two
scenes share a shape or motion.
GraphicsHook title, kinetic captions, score or product cards, logo placement, end card with a
call to action.
AudioLicensed music cut to the beat grid, natural sound cleaned, music ducked under
speech, sound design on transitions, loudness mastered for the platform.
Quality
control
No black or frozen frames, no colour jump between cuts, text inside safe zones and
readable on a phone, logo present, length within spec, no rapid flashing.
When editing can't save a scene, the fallbacks run in order: use a shorter sharp section → relight or
reframe → regenerate from the best frame → a film-it card → drop the scene and re-plan around it.
